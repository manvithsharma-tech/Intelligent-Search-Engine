Improving weight loss among young adults with excess body weight using a theory-driven digital self-regulation program: a randomized controlled trial.

Lifestyle modification is fundamental for obesity management but remains challenging. To test the effectiveness of a 12-week digital self-regulation program on improving weight loss and self-regulation among young adults. A 2-arm parallel 1:1 randomized controlled trial was conducted. Participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention or the control group. The primary outcomes were weight and self-regulation, while the secondary outcomes were nutrition knowledge, eating phenotypes, overeating habits, anxiety, and depression. All data were collected at weeks 0, 12, and 24. We showed the effectiveness of a digital commitment device on body weight (-1.90 ± 6.4 kg, P = .002), body mass index (-0.45 ± 2.0 kg/m2, P = .002), waist circumference (-2.10 ± 12.1 cm, P = .01), basal metabolic rate (-20.30 ± 118.6 kcal, P = .04) and body roundness index (-0.17 ± 0.6, P = .014). Interventional effects were sustained for waist circumference (-0.8 ± 2.8 cm, P = .05) and body roundness index (-0.07 ± 0.36, P = .047) 3 months beyond removing access to the commitment device. General improvements in nutrition knowledge, psychological flexibility, uncontrolled eating, and anxiety were found among both groups, but differences were only significant for depression scores (-0.06 ± 1.5, P = .032) and eating self-regulation (0.16 ± 0.54, P = .003). This study showed the potential of a digital commitment device in improving eating self-regulation, anthropometry, and mental wellness for scalable implementation in the community setting. Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05969639). Many young adults struggle with weight management, even when they are motivated to eat healthily. One reason is that everyday stress, food temptations, and busy schedules make it hard to stick to good intentions. This study tested whether a smartphone app designed to strengthen self-regulation skills could help young adults with excess body weight manage their weight more effectively. We conducted a randomized controlled trial in which participants were assigned either to a 12-week digital self-regulation program or to a control group that did not receive the app. The app encouraged users to reflect on their eating habits, manage emotional triggers, and plan ahead for challenging situations, rather than focusing only on calorie counting. Participants who used the app lost more weight by the end of the 12-week program compared with those who did not use it. Interestingly, improvements in eating self-regulation appeared later, 3 months after the program ended, suggesting that these skills may take time to develop. The app also had a low dropout rate, indicating that it was acceptable and easy to use. Overall, this study suggests that digital programs focusing on self-regulation skills may offer a practical and scalable way to support healthy weight management in young adults.
